Fraternity leaders put a pillowcase over Colin Martinez’s head and walked him into the Tau Delta fraternity house in Flagstaff, Arizona. Throughout the night he was forced to drink half a handle of vodka as part of an initiation ritual. He never walked out of the house. 

Martinez’s blood alcohol level was more than five times the legal limit when he was pronounced dead at the frat house in January, according to a coroner's report released in March.

The 18-year-old college freshman rushed the Tau Delta this semester at Northern Arizona University, where senior frat leaders forced him and three other pledges to drink two bottles of vodka, enough alcohol to make each of them puke. Frat members spent the night readjusting Martinez’s body, checking his pulse and Googling signs of alcohol poisoning. No one called for help until the next morning.

Now, Martinez is dead and one frat leader is facing a criminal charge for the fatal hazing. 
This is just the latest instance of a lethal tradition among college fraternities and sororities. 
“The nature and extent of hazing is far more widespread than most people anticipate,” said Elizabeth Allan, hazing researcher at the University of Maine. 

Hazing is any humiliating, degrading, or dangerous activity required to join or maintain membership in a group, regardless of the person's willingness to participate. Often, senior members of an organization haze new members to assert hierarchy. 
Over half of all college students involved in college organizations, including Greek life, clubs or sports, have experienced hazing, according to Allan’s research. Still, nearly 95% of hazing cases go unreported. 

In extreme cases, hazing turns deadly. 

Since the turn of the century, 127 people have died in hazing-related incidents, according to the U.S. Hazing Death Database maintained by Hazinginfo.org. College aged students, 18 to 22-years-old, are the most likely group to die from the rituals, but hazing is a persistent issue across all age groups and geographies in the U.S.

Jolayne Houtz founded Hazinginfo.org in 2023 to make information about the potentially violent past of Greek life organizations easier to find for parents of college students. Her son Sam Martinez died from a hazing incident at Washington State University in 2019. 

“My son would still be alive if I had access to even a portion of what I later learned about Washington State University and Alpha Tau Omega, the fraternity he was pledging,” Houtz said. 

She successfully lobbied the state of Washington to pass a law making hazing a felony after her son died of alcohol poisoning, with a blood-alcohol level nearly five times the legal limit.

Forced substance consumption is the leading cause of death for fatal hazing incidents in the United States, an analysis of deadly hazing incidents shows. Almost all states have anti-hazing laws, but only a dozen or so classify hazing as a felony. 
Hazing has killed at least one person in America every year since 1959. Most often, college aged men are the victims. 
Houtz started the hazing deaths database, which is just a portion of information on Hazinginfo.org, with the help of Hank Nuwer, a journalist who has been tracking hazing incidents for decades. She said fatal incidents are added based on news coverage and independent confirmation.

She and other parents of hazing victims pushed the U.S. Congress to pass an anti-hazing law in 2024, which mandates public universities in the U.S. publish annual reports of hazing incidents and implement anti-hazing education programs. That requirement started in 2025. As of January, only 46% of schools nationwide are in compliance. 

Allan, the hazing researcher, said the federal law is a huge step towards accountability, but still, more needs to be done to educate students on the power dynamics of college clubs, she said. 

“We need people to pay attention,” Allan said. 

The most common cause of death in fatal hazings are toxicological, like alcohol or drug toxicity, which is listed as the primary or secondary cause of death in 49.6% of the 127 deadly hazing incidents reported since 2000. Blunt force trauma follows closely behind, being named in 44.2% of cases.

Pennsylvania State University freshman Tim Piazza died from a mixture of both while rushing Beta Theta Pi in the Spring of 2017. 

He was forced to down 18 drinks in just one hour, a mixture of vodka and beer, in the frat house basement. Tim was so drunk he couldn’t stand up straight. His fellow pledges carried him to the couch as he hunched over, trying to stay upright.

He tried to leave the basement multiple times into the night, falling on wooden and concrete floors. He would eventually stumble his way up a few stairs, only to fall fifteen feet, lacerating his spleen and cracking his skull. 

Frat members rough-housed around him, one stepping over his body on the way to the kitchen. No one called for help until the next afternoon. Tim died days later in a local hospital, hooked up to a ventilator.

Nearly ten years later, walking through the grocery store his mother Evelyn still has a hard time seeing chocolate poptarts, which were Tim’s favorite snack. In the almost decade since their son died, Evelyn and Tim’s father, Jim, have advocated for harsher hazing laws in the U.S. 

“The thing that really grinds us, is that (Tim’s death) was needless. It didn't have to happen,” Jim said. “It was pure negligence on the part of other people.” 

The Pennsylvania legislature passed the Timothy J. Piazza Anti-Hazing Law in 2018, which bolstered punishments for hazing, including classifying hazing that results in bodily harm as a third degree felony. That law isn’t enough if police don’t use the law to punish abusers, Jim said. 

Despite the law, Pennsylvania police have yet to arrest or charge anyone in connection with a near fatal hazing incident that happened nearly six months ago at Rutgers University.

A 19-year-old student rushing Alpha Sigma Phi was electrocuted during a hazing ritual, according to an investigation by the university, leaving him in critical condition and hospitalizing him for months. The school banned the university from campus, but no criminal charges have been filed. 

The Piazzas also had a hand in drafting the federal anti-hazing law passed in 2024. It’s unrealistic to think Greek organizations will ever stop existing, Evelyn said, because of powerful alumni with large financial backing. She called Greek life “big business.” 

Instead of eradication, the Piazza’s are pushing for accountability. Jim said police need to apply the existing laws, national fraternities need to educate better and universities have to hold students accountable. 

While fraternities and sororities will never cease to exist, Evelyn said it’s important to increase hazing education and hold people accountable for violating laws. 

“No good comes from hazing. The whole point of it is to cause harm. It’s inherently risky,” Evelyn said. “Eventually, somebody will pay the price.”
